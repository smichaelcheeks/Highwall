# Regional Hydrology

## Purpose

Record the author's current understanding of the regional hydrology surrounding Highwall. This seed establishes the broad behavior of water across the setting and its relationship to previously established geology. It intentionally avoids defining detailed infrastructure, named waterways beyond existing working names, or exact hydrogeologic mechanisms unless explicitly confirmed.

## Scope

This seed covers:

- regional drainage
- seasonal water sources
- Dryrun
- groundwater as it relates to the sandstone formations
- springs and seeps
- the collapsed-lake basin
- the hidden agricultural basin beyond the Whitestone rim

This seed does **not** establish:

- Highwall's engineered water system
- exact rainfall amounts
- exact river discharge
- exact aquifer structure
- ecology
- irrigation
- historical flood chronology
- names of additional rivers or tributaries

---

## Confirmed Setting Truths

The surrounding plateau is semi-arid rather than a true desert.

Water availability changes significantly throughout the year.

The primary seasonal water inputs are:

- late-summer and autumn storms;
- winter snowfall;
- spring snowmelt.

Highwall is built within a narrow sandstone canyon.

The canyon contains a river currently referred to in development discussions as **Dryrun**. This remains a working name.

Dryrun is intended to be a seasonal or intermittent river rather than a large permanently flowing river.

Dryrun is capable of producing destructive flash floods despite often carrying relatively little visible water.

Several flash floods occur during a typical year. Most are expected and are part of the normal environmental rhythm of the canyon.

Near the canyon mouth, Dryrun joins a larger river which flows year-round and eventually reaches the sea.

The plateau contains substantial sandstone capable of storing groundwater. The author intends groundwater behavior to be broadly plausible for porous sandstone rather than fantastical.

Springs and seeps exist throughout the region.

The ancient collapsed-lake basin no longer contains a permanent lake and is instead characterized by extensive salt flats.

Highwall possesses access to a hidden enclosed basin beyond the Whitestone rim. This basin retains sufficient water to support limited reserve agriculture.

---

## Relationships to Existing Geology

Regional hydrology is intended to arise naturally from the established geology rather than exist independently of it.

Water movement is expected to be strongly influenced by:

- the resistant Whitestone cap;
- the thick underlying Redstone;
- canyon incision;
- seasonal precipitation;
- groundwater moving through sandstone.

The exact hydrogeologic behavior of Whitestone and Redstone has not yet been established.

---

## Dryrun

Dryrun is intended to appear unreliable during much of the year while remaining capable of becoming extremely dangerous during flood events.

The author's intent is that the apparent contradiction between "normally dry" and "frequently flooding" is a defining characteristic of Highwall.

The exact amount of perennial flow has not been established.

The exact relationship between visible surface flow and subsurface groundwater has not been established.

The final name of the river has not been established.

---

## Groundwater

Groundwater exists within the regional sandstone.

The author intends this groundwater to behave in a manner broadly inspired by real sandstone aquifer systems.

Groundwater contributes to springs and seeps.

The exact groundwater architecture has not been established.

Not established:

- whether Whitestone and Redstone function as separate aquifers;
- perched groundwater systems;
- confining layers;
- groundwater residence times;
- recharge rates;
- water table depths;
- groundwater chemistry.

---

## Springs and Seeps

Springs and seeps are an expected feature of the canyon landscape.

Some are expected to be permanent while others may vary seasonally.

The author has not yet established:

- their distribution;
- their reliability;
- their importance to settlement;
- their relationship to specific geological contacts.

---

## Seasonal Water

Winter snowfall is intended to be an important component of the regional water cycle.

Spring snowmelt provides a significant seasonal water source.

Late-summer and autumn storms are expected to produce many of the region's most dangerous floods.

The relative contribution of rainfall, snowmelt, and groundwater to annual water supply has not been established.

---

## Flooding

Flash flooding is a defining environmental hazard for Highwall.

Multiple flash floods are expected during most years and are a normal feature of the canyon environment.

Floods usually originate from storms occurring upstream rather than over the city itself.

The author intends flood danger to result from the interaction of regional watershed behavior and Highwall's canyon setting.

Specific flood mechanics have not yet been established.

---

## Collapsed-Lake Basin

The former lake no longer exists as a permanent body of water.

The basin now contains extensive salt flats.

The surrounding terrain includes mountains and canyon-cut uplands.

Fresh water is expected to occur primarily around the basin margins rather than across the central salt flats.

The exact hydrologic relationship between the basin and Dryrun has not been established.

---

## Hidden Basin

Highwall has access to a hidden enclosed basin beyond the Whitestone rim.

Highwall discovered this basin after excavation unexpectedly broke through into it.

Nearby highland canyon clans were already aware of the basin and knew natural routes into it.

The basin retains sufficient moisture to support limited reserve agriculture.

The exact hydrologic mechanism responsible for retaining water has not been established.

---

## Author Intent

The author intends the regional hydrology to feel scientifically plausible while remaining understandable to readers.

Water should be scarce enough to influence settlement, travel, trade, and politics without making the region uninhabitable.

The setting should naturally support prolonged dry periods, regular seasonal flash floods, and occasional exceptionally destructive flood events.

Hydrology should emerge from previously established geology rather than requiring supernatural explanations.

## Proposals Under Consideration

The author has discussed, but not yet established:

- possible historical changes to drainage associated with the collapse of the ancient lake.

These ideas remain proposals until confirmed elsewhere.
