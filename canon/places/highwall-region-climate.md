# Climate of the Highwall Region

## Regional pattern

The region is predominantly semi-arid, but climate varies substantially with
elevation and distance from the western sea. The coast is significantly wetter
than the plateau. The coastal escarpment removes much of the moisture from
systems moving inland and places the plateau in a rain shadow.

Annual precipitation varies considerably, and multi-year droughts occur. Most
precipitation arrives through two seasonal moisture systems: cool-season
atmospheric rivers from the western sea and warm-season moisture transported
northward from tropical waters south of the region.

## Climatic zones

The [Stormlands](stormlands.md) receive the region's greatest winter
precipitation. Frequent winter rain sustains their extensive forests and, in
turn, the timber resources used in construction and shipbuilding.

The western plateau is semi-arid and receives substantially less precipitation
than the coast because of the rain shadow. The eastern plateau is also
semi-arid, but its late-summer storm season is more dependable.

The eastern mountains receive the region's greatest snowfall, accumulate a
substantial seasonal snowpack, supply most spring snowmelt, and promote
late-summer thunderstorm development.

The inland basin, also called the **Salt Basin**, remains dry through much of
the year. During storm season,
temporary freshwater runoff descends from the surrounding mountains. Seasonal
flooding creates temporary wetlands around the basin margins before dry
conditions return.

## Annual seasonal cycle

### Winter

Cool-season storm systems arrive from the western sea. They bring frequent
rain to the Stormlands, then lose much of their moisture while crossing the
coastal escarpment. The plateau therefore receives considerably less winter
precipitation. The eastern mountains accumulate seasonal snow. Some winter
precipitation reaches the plateau and Highwall, although snow on the canyon
floor is uncommon.

### Spring

Snowmelt from the eastern mountains makes river flow more dependable and
increases groundwater recharge. Spring is the most reliable period for surface
water availability.

### Early summer

Temperatures rise and much of the plateau dries. Travel conditions are
generally favorable, while water becomes increasingly dependent on springs,
stored supplies, and remaining streamflow.

### Late summer and early autumn

Heating over the interior draws warm, humid air northward from tropical waters
south of the region, beginning **storm season**. Thunderstorms become common
across the eastern plateau, mountain front, and upper watersheds.

Storms are frequently localized: one watershed may receive heavy rain while a
neighboring watershed receives little or none. Multiple flash floods occur in
a typical year. They usually originate in upstream storms, so flooding can
reach Highwall even when little or no rain falls over the city.

### Late autumn

Storm frequency gradually declines, temperatures cool, and surface water
transitions toward winter conditions.

## Storm season and regional life

Storm season is a defining regional cycle. It strongly influences travel,
trade, seasonal labor, pastoral movement, Blueleaf harvests, reed cultivation,
and flood preparedness. Blueleaf harvesting and reed cultivation are
established activities, but this climate relationship does not establish their
detailed practices or the biological details of the organisms involved. Those
organism-level details remain working canon unless separately established.

## Highwall Canyon microclimates

Highwall Canyon's north-south orientation produces pronounced daily and
seasonal cycles of sunlight and shade. The plateau surface and canyon rims are
generally more exposed to wind, while the canyon interior is more sheltered.
These differences make conditions on the canyon floor noticeably different
from those above.

Across the region, elevation, slope orientation, and exposure produce
substantial local variation in snowfall, snow retention, moisture, and growing
conditions. Wind exposure is a practical consideration for travel,
construction, and daily life.

## Relationship to regional water

Climate drives the [regional hydrologic cycle](highwall-region-hydrology.md).
Winter snow supplies spring snowmelt, late-summer storms produce recurring
flash floods, and variable precipitation contributes to recurring droughts.
Mountain snowfall and storm formation affect downstream water availability.

## Open issues

growing-season lengths, severe winter-storm frequency, hidden-basin climate,
detailed canyon wind cycles, marine-fog penetration, atmospheric-river
frequency and strength, prevailing winds outside the established seasonal
patterns, and the exact storm-belt extent remain [unestablished](../../development/open-questions/regional-climate-details.md).
